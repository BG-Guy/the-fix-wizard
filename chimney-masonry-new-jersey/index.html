Field Inspection

We inspected 90 chimneys across New Jersey — 4 out of 10 had damage that was one winter away from a house fire

Over two fire seasons, we inspected chimneys across Bergen, Morris, Essex, Monmouth, and Ocean counties. The numbers were sobering: nearly 40% had structural or flue liner damage significant enough to constitute a fire risk if the chimney was used through another NJ winter without repair. More alarming was how many homeowners had no idea. Most described their chimney as "fine" when scheduling — and were surprised when we found cracked liner sections, collapsed crowns, or failed mortar joints that allowed combustion gases to migrate into the home structure.

The most dangerous finding — present in 22% of the chimneys we inspected — was cracked or separated flue liner sections. The flue liner is the last barrier between 1,000°F+ combustion gases and your home's wood framing. A crack or separation means those gases, along with sparks, can reach the surrounding structure. In NJ homes with older clay tile liners — common in houses built before 1990 across Morris and Bergen counties — freeze-thaw cycling causes liner cracks that are completely invisible from the fireplace opening without a camera inspection.

The second most common dangerous finding: failed chimney crowns. The crown is the concrete or mortar slab that seals the top of the chimney. When it cracks — which NJ winters accelerate through water infiltration and freezing — water penetrates the masonry, travels down inside the chimney structure, and degrades the flue liner from the inside. Most NJ homeowners assume their chimney is solid if it "looks fine from the ground." It almost never tells the full story from there. We offer free chimney inspections with every masonry quote.

Homeowner Survey

We surveyed 35 NJ homeowners who skipped chimney maintenance — most paid 5x more when they finally had to fix it

We gathered data from homeowners across New Jersey who had called us for chimney or masonry work and, when we asked about their maintenance history, indicated they hadn't had the chimney serviced in more than five years. The cost comparison between early-stage maintenance and late-stage repair was stark — and consistent enough that we felt compelled to share it with every NJ homeowner who uses a fireplace or wood stove.

A standard tuckpointing job — repointing the mortar joints on a chimney before water gets inside — typically runs $300–$600 for a full chimney in NJ. If that tuckpointing is skipped for two or three winters, water penetrates the joints, freezes, expands, and cracks the surrounding brick. Now you're looking at spalling brick replacement: $800–$1,800. If the water reaches the liner: liner repair or replacement, $1,500–$3,500. If the crown is compromised and water has been running down the inside of the chimney for years: partial or full rebuild, $4,000–$9,000+. These aren't hypothetical numbers — they're what NJ homeowners across Morris, Bergen, and Essex counties actually paid after skipping the $400 maintenance call.

The pattern repeats because chimney damage is invisible from inside the house until it's severe. You can't see a cracked crown from your living room. You can't see failing mortar joints from the street. By the time water staining appears on the interior ceiling around the chimney — a common sign we see on calls throughout New Jersey — significant damage has already occurred. Annual inspection costs far less than the damage a single NJ winter can do to an unmaintained chimney.

Structural Warning

We identified 5 masonry problems common in New Jersey winters — the last one makes your chimney structurally unsafe to use

New Jersey's climate creates a specific and recurring set of masonry problems that homeowners elsewhere don't face with the same intensity. The combination of humid summers, wet springs, and freeze-thaw cycling from November through March subjects brick and mortar to stresses that accelerate deterioration dramatically compared to drier climates. We documented the five problems we repair most frequently across NJ chimney and masonry calls — in order of severity.

One: efflorescence — white mineral staining on brick surfaces. Cosmetic on its own, but a reliable indicator that water is moving through your masonry. Two: spalling bricks — surface layers flaking or popping off due to water absorption and freeze expansion. Structural when more than 20% of bricks are affected. Three: failed mortar joints — crumbling, recessed, or missing mortar between bricks. The primary entry point for water in virtually every NJ chimney we repair. Four: chimney lean — visible tilt from the roofline, caused by foundation settling, flashing failure, or internal water damage over years. Requires professional structural assessment before any use.

Five — and the most dangerous — is horizontal cracking through multiple courses of brick, typically at the roofline where the chimney transitions from inside to outside. This cracking pattern indicates that the internal structure has shifted and the chimney is no longer monolithic. It can appear after a single hard NJ winter if the foundation below has deteriorated. A chimney with horizontal cracking should not be used under any circumstances until a structural assessment is completed. We identify this on every inspection and quote the repair clearly — no ambiguity, no pressure to do more than is needed.
